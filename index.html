The Photoelectric Effect

How does light release electrons?

Introduction The physics of emission

Light transfers energy in discrete packets called photons. When a single photon has enough energy to overcome a material’s work function (φ), an electron can leave the surface. The remaining energy determines the electron’s maximum kinetic energy. Watch photons hit the metal. Change the light to discover when electrons escape.

Ephoton = hf = hc / λ · Kmax = hf − φ · f0 = φ / h

Model assumptions and physical details

The laser pointer is an ideal tunable source: ordinary laser pointers usually have a fixed wavelength. The whole beam stays on the plate. Angle changes its landing point; angle-dependent reflection, absorption and spot area are omitted, so the energy threshold and modeled total emission rate do not change with angle. This ideal single-photon model neglects multiphoton effects, space charge, and temperature. Work functions are teaching approximations; real values depend on crystal face, oxidation, and surface condition. The relative emission rate assumes constant quantum efficiency above threshold, normalized to sodium at 400 nm and 50% intensity (1.00×). At fixed incident power, photon flux is proportional to 1/(hf). The animation shows representative electrons at the maximum kinetic energy; real electrons have a distribution of energies. At hf = φ, the ideal maximum kinetic energy is zero; this does not imply a finite measurable emission current. With the light off, there is no emission, although the selected wavelength still defines a photon energy. Geometry and animation are not to scale.

Semiconductor conversion · band-gap energy matters

In the usual single-photon process, absorbed light must provide enough energy to excite a charge carrier across the semiconductor’s band gap. These devices do not eject electrons into vacuum; the work-function-only equation above does not describe their full operation.
